OUR FOUNDATION

Neither Text Alone, Nor Instruction Alone

Our discipleship training is built on a single conviction: the Word and the Spirit of God work together. Scripture alone, read without the Spirit's illumination, can become mere information. The Spirit's leading, untethered from Scripture, can drift from the truth. Together, they form and sanctify a life.

This is the heart of sanctification — not a program to complete, but a lifelong process of being made holy, taught by the Word and led by the Spirit, in the company of others walking the same road.

“All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work.”
2 Timothy 3:16–17 (ESV)
HOW WE TRAIN

Three Commitments

Every part of our discipleship training reflects these three commitments.

Word-Centered

Scripture is read directly and carefully, allowing the Bible to speak for itself rather than leaning primarily on theological commentary.

Spirit-Led

Growth is not merely intellectual. We seek the Spirit's work in conviction, understanding, and transformation as we study and pray together.

Community-Formed

Sanctification is not a solitary pursuit. Discipleship happens in relationship — mentored, accountable, and shared across cultures and generations.

THE METHOD IN ACTION

Three Ways to Practice

Howard Hendricks taught that personal Bible study is a methodicalness — specific steps, in a specific order, producing a specific result: Observation (“What do I see?”), Interpretation (“What does it mean?”), and Application (“How does it work?”). Here are three passages, each used a different way, so you can see the method, try it with help, and then try it on your own.

Choose your Bible version: Your choice stays consistent across all three exercises below.
① STEP-BY-STEP EXAMPLE — READ THROUGH IT
John 15:1-8
1. Observation
What do I see?
  • Jesus makes an “I am” statement: “I am the true vine.”
  • Three roles are named: the vine (Jesus), the branches (his followers), and the farmer (the Father).
  • Two things happen to branches: some are “taken away,” others are “pruned.”
  • One word repeats more than any other: “remain” (also translated “abide”).
  • The passage ends by tying fruit to two things: the Father's glory, and being called a disciple.
2. Interpretation
What does it mean?

A branch has no life source of its own — cut off from the vine, it doesn't just grow slower, it has nothing at all. “Remain” describes a settled, ongoing connection, not a single decision made once. Pruning is not punishment; it's the Father's positive work, aimed at more fruit, not less. And fruit itself isn't something a branch produces by effort — it's the natural, visible result of staying attached to the vine.

3. Application
How does it work?

“Abiding” is a daily practice — ongoing time in the Word and prayer, not sporadic effort. It reframes a hard season: a difficult pruning may not be punishment at all, but the Father shaping you toward more fruit. And it shifts the starting question from “How do I produce results?” to “Am I staying connected?” A question worth sitting with: where in your life right now are you trying to bear fruit without staying connected to the vine?

② GUIDED PRACTICE — CHOOSE AN ANSWER FOR EACH STEP
Psalm 119:9-11
Observation — What repeated idea connects all three verses?
“Word” and “heart” each appear across these three verses — the passage is built around where God's word is kept: not just known, but held in the heart.
Interpretation — According to this passage, how does someone avoid moral failure?
The psalmist's own answer is explicit: “By living according to your word” — and the method is named directly: hiding the word in the heart.
Application — What is the most direct, practical way to live out “I have hidden your word in my heart”?
“Hidden in the heart” describes Scripture that's memorized — carried with you, present in a moment of temptation whether or not a physical Bible is at hand.
③ DO IT YOURSELF — THEN CHECK YOUR WORK
James 1:22-25
1. Observation — What do I see?
2. Interpretation — What does it mean?
3. Application — How does it work?
A SUGGESTED ANSWER — NOT THE ONLY RIGHT ONE
Observation

Two people are compared: a “hearer” and a “doer.” The hearer is compared to a man who looks in a mirror and “immediately forgets.” The law is called “perfect” and connected to “freedom.” The doer is called “blessed.”

Interpretation

James isn't condemning hearing — hearing is the assumed starting point. The danger is stopping there, treating the Word like a mirror glance that's forgotten the moment you look away. The doer “continues” — a lasting engagement, not a single moment of obedience.

Application

A simple self-check: after reading Scripture this week, did you close the book and move on unaffected — like the man who forgets his own reflection? Or did something you read lead to an actual changed action, decision, or conversation?

THE GROWTH PATHWAY

A Path of Formation

Our discipleship training is being developed as a structured pathway — from foundational study, into mentored growth, toward a life that multiplies into others.

01

Foundations

Core Scripture study — who God is, who we are, and the shape of a life under the Word.

02

Formation

Mentored growth — walking with a guide, being shaped by the Spirit through prayer, study, and honest accountability.

03

Multiplication

A mature life that disciples others — carrying the Word and Spirit's work into a new generation and new cultures.

The full curriculum for each stage is currently in development. Details on course content, format, and schedule will be added here as they are finalized.
RECOMMENDED RESOURCES

Tools for Personal Bible Study

A short, growing list of resources we recommend as you build your own habit of personal Bible study.

01
Living By the Book
Howard G. Hendricks & William D. Hendricks

For decades, Dr. Howard Hendricks taught Bible Study Methods to more than 30,000 students at Dallas Theological Seminary — a course his students often called life-changing. This book distills that teaching into a simple, three-step method for personal Bible study: Observation (“What do I see?”), Interpretation (“What does it mean?”), and Application (“How does it work?”).

“This book will keep you from sin, or sin will keep you from this book.”
02
The Spirit of the Disciplines
Dallas Willard

Willard, a philosopher and theologian, wrote this book to answer a question too many Christians never ask: how does a life actually come to look like Christ's? His answer centers on spiritual disciplines — not as rule-keeping, but as practices that position a person to be shaped by the Holy Spirit over time.

03
Renovation of the Heart
Dallas Willard

If The Spirit of the Disciplines explains the practices that open a life to the Spirit's work, Renovation of the Heart maps out what that work actually changes: thought, feeling, will, body, social relationships, and the soul that holds them all together. Willard's subtitle says it plainly — putting on the character of Christ.

04
The Naked Bible Podcast
Dr. Michael S. Heiser

Dr. Heiser, a scholar of the ancient Near East and biblical languages, built this podcast around a simple discipline: strip away the filters of denominational tradition, modern culture, and personal experience, and read the Bible the way its original authors and audience actually understood it. That “naked” encounter with the text is exactly what raises awareness of how much the Bible actually says — how much gets missed when we default to reading it through our own time and culture instead of its own. Dr. Heiser passed away in 2023, but the podcast continues today, carried on by fellow scholars in the same spirit.

“Biblical theology, stripped bare of denominational confessions and theological systems by exposing context.”
05
Supernatural
Dr. Michael S. Heiser

This is Dr. Heiser's accessible companion to his more academic work, written for the person in the pew rather than the scholar or seminarian. It covers the same core discovery — how much of the Bible's own worldview gets lost when modern readers bring modern assumptions to the text instead of the ancient context its authors actually shared — in plain language, without footnotes or technical argument standing in the way. Reading it is itself an exercise in taking the Word seriously enough to read it on its own terms.

More resources will be added here as this list grows.
